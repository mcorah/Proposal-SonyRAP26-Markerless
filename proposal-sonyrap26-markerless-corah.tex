\section{Abstract}

% concise summary of objectives methodology, and expected outcomes

The advent of technologies such as Gaussian Splatting promise a future where
large static and dynamic scenes can routinely be captured and reconstructed for
the purpose of photorealistic rendering or for analysis of biomechanics and
motion.
However, current methods for motion capture involve installing large
arrays of cameras to cover limited capture volumes.
Instead, we propose development of methods for low-cost, high-capture-volume
markerless motion capture based controlling fewer cameras
(such as pan-tilt-zoom units) to densely cover relevant parts of a dynamic
scene.
Our methods will enable tracking and capture of multiple subjects moving about a
large space such as a soccer field or a running track.
To do so, we will leverage methods we have recently developed for coordinating
multiple cameras to observe dynamic scenes---methods that enable coverage of
groups of subjects as they move together or apart or reorganize over the
duration of a task.
We propose combining these methods of camera coordination to maximize visual
coverage with methods for kinematic and dense reconstruction.
Dense and kinematic reconstruction methods developed through this work will be
evaluated via laboratory and field experiments and with
comparison to static and marker-based camera systems.
We plan to release key components of the system as open-source software and will
publish results in premier robotics conferences.

\section{Introduction}

Existing markerless motion capture systems rely on arrays of static cameras
to reconstruct human motion in a designated capture volume.
Capturing motion of groups of people or athletes \emph{in situ} (e.g. during a
sporting event) as they move about in a large space (such as a football field)
becomes prohibitively challenging---particularly for dense capture---due to
increased requirements for numbers of cameras and amounts of infrastructure to
cover the large capture volume.
To address this challenge, we propose integrating methods for autonomous
coordination of mulitiple cameras for filming groups of moving subjects with
methods for reconstruction and markerless motion capture.
The proposed system, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:markerless_system}, will
enable inexpensive markerless motion capture in large spaces
by coordinating multiple pan-tilt-zoom cameras to film and reconstruct
groups of moving subjects.

\subsection{Background}

Over the past six years, markerless motion capture has transitioned from
research topic to product, and software and hardware offerings have emerged,
often from leading manufacturers of marker-based systems, e.g. Vicon, Optitrack,
or Qualisys.
% Could add more detail here on biomechanics data collection
These systems promise applications from walk-in virtual reality
worlds\footnote{%
  Vicon demo ``Clockwork Forest'' presented at SIGGRAPH 2023:
  \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3Ex4XwBqvjA}
}
to collecting biomechanics data for medical evaluations with minimal
time and preparation~\citep{wade2022peerj}.
Yet, like their marker-based cousins, these systems consist of large numbers of
cameras (e.g. 8+) that must be carefully located, mounted to tripods or a
truss system, and rigorously calibrated.
The goal of this project is then to \textbf{prototype and spur
development of a new class of low-cost and unobtrusive markerless motion capture
systems that can be readily deployed to cover large open spaces such as sports
facilities and arenas.}

\micah{Some of this should maybe be gap text}
The research community meanwhile has sought to cast away the anchor of
fixed infrastructure by developing markerless motion capture capabilities around
formations of UAVs~\citep{saini2019iccv,ho2021iros,hong2024eccv,saini2022ral}.
While these aerial systems would not promise accuracy comparable to systems
built around fixed cameras with calibrated extrinsics, they instead promise the
ability to reconstruct human motion outdoors and largely without bounds.
However, these systems have another, less obvious, limitation: research on
aerial markerless motion capture has largely focused only on reconstructing
motions of individual people and neglects to address reconstruction of motions
of groups.
Further, because these approaches generally rely on robots that fly in a
circular formation around one subject, capturing multiple subjects, especially
if those subjects may sometimes move apart, poses a significant challenge
regarding view planning and coordination.

Alternatively, systems based on static pan-tilt-zoom
cameras~\citep{bachmann20193dv,yamamoto2022visigrapp}
could strike a balance and provide benefits of camera coordination
(e.g. operation in large and outdoor spaces) without limitations of aerial
systems.
Yet, there has been limited interest in developing such
systems, and these prior works exhibit some of the same limitations as aerial
systems such as limitation in scope to capturing motion of only a single
subject.
\emph{Therefore, a key focus of our work will be to introduce methods for camera
coordination for filming multiple moving subjects that we have recently
proposed~\citep{hughes2024cdc,suresh2024iros,bouy2026}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:multi-robot-videography}) and apply these methods to the
development of methods for markerless motion capture.}

\subsection{Impacts}

We expect our work to lead to development and proliferation of
low-cost, low-infrastructure markerless motion capture with widespread
impacts spanning sports, entertainment, and movement sciences---for Sony,
opportunities to develop new products and expand markets.
These systems would make \emph{in situ} markerless motion capture viable in
domains and regimes where doing so would otherwise be challenging or infeasible
due to cost and the scale of the capture volume
\emph{transforming the economics of motion capture across scales and domains}.
For \textbf{kinematic motion capture}, the systems we propose would enable
motion capture at the scale of a sporting field with very small numbers of
cameras (e.g. 4--6) and could become accessible to amateur and semi-professional
athletes and researchers.
Regarding \textbf{dense motion capture}, systems that perform dense capture at
the scale of a stadium may require hundreds of cameras---improving targeting via
camera coordination will enable capture at higher resolution and with less
infrastructure.

For \textbf{team sports}, motion capture data may augment practice film and
improve coaching and analysis and is already being used to automate refereering.
\micah{Was there another paper I remembered on injury detection?}
Motion capture is also highly relevant to
\textbf{movement sciences}~\citep{wade2022peerj,nakano2020} and in turn can lead
to improvements in rehabilitation and injury prevention.
For \textbf{entertainment purposes}, dense reconstruction of sporting events
and performances could enable synthesis of novel views and video streams or
enable development of new AR or VR applications.
Regarding \textbf{animation}, larger volumes and reduced costs and
infrastructure may increase the scope and scale of use of motion capture data.
Finally, proposed methods will enable development of human motion capture
datasets for \textbf{training machine learning models} that feature greater
diversity of movement, interactions between multiple subjects, and minimally
invasive methods; such datasets could improve humanoid robot motion control or
models for digital humans.

\paragraph{Camera coordination challenges}

\begin{enumerate}[resume,itemsep=0.3ex,topsep=0.3ex]
  \item Distributing camera views over subjects and obtaining observations from
    multiple perspectives
  \item Maintaining coverage of multiple subjects and groups that spread out and
    rearrange
  \item Achieving adequate coverage and detail for the desired reconstruction
    task (kinematic pose, dense reconstruction)
\end{enumerate}
The requirements for capturing motions of multiple subjects such as athletes
suggest developing methods suitable to coordinate cameras to observe all
subjects at once and from multiple perspectives.
Further, while we propose evaluation by reconstructing kinematic pose, we seek
to develop systems suitable for future application for dense
reconstruction.
Therefore, the capacity to optimize camera zoom to increase resolution
over the surface of relevant portions of the subjects and scene becomes
paramount for potential downstream applications, e.g. synthesizing a novel and
photorealistic video feed as an entertainment technology.

\paragraph{Systems and integration challenges}

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.3ex,topsep=0.3ex]
  \item Scaling of infrastructure requirements with capture volume
  \item Identifying and tracking subjects and forecasting motion
  \item Uncertainty in intrinsics and extrinsics
\end{enumerate}
The key practical challenge we are interested in is that conventional motion
capture systems rely on fixed camera configurations to produce extensive and
multiple coverage over a capture volume---while fixed configurations may be
economical for small, often indoor, volumes scaling to large and outdoor spaces,
e.g. for \emph{in situ} capture of sporting events, leads to prohibitive costs
as requirements for numbers of cameras and the size of their support structure
multiply many times over.
Further, intensification of infrastructure requirements also produces secondary
conflicts such as obscuring spectators' views of athletes.

However, dispensing with fixed cameras
%and adopting controlled or mobile cameras
leads to new technical challenges.
Identifying subjects and tracking and forecasting locations becomes critical
to the goal of coordination as pictured in Fig.~\ref{fig:markerless_system}.
Likewise, adjusting camera orientation and zoom while simultaneously collecting
data to reconstruct subjects' motions undermines requirements for precise
extrinsic and intrinsic calibration.
This will necessitate development of motion capture methods that adapt
calibration continuously---estimated via feedback from mechanical encoders and
fine-tuned via image registration---or adopting methods amenable to uncalibrated
systems~\citep{bachmann20193dv,xu2022bmvc}.

\subsection{Gap: Robotic markerless motion capture}

\paragraph{Aerial motion capture}
Although systems for marker-based and markerless motion capture are typically
built around static cameras, there has recently been some interest in developing
markerless motion capture systems based around mobile, especially aerial,
cameras~\citep{ho2021iros,saini2022ral,saini2019iccv,hong2024eccv,zhou2018icra}.
All demonstrate feasibility of reconstructing human skeletal motion via data
produced by camera-equipped aerial vehicles, often via gimbal cameras with zoom
function.
Moreover, these methods enable motion capture in outdoor spaces and with loose
bounds on the subject's motion.
However, these methods are subject to significant technical and operational
limitations:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.3ex,topsep=0.3ex]
  \item Limited number of tracked subjects
  \item Limited coordination of camera motion and zoom
  \item Operational constraints: battery life, safety, and policy
\end{enumerate}

The referenced works on aerial motion capture all focus on reconstructing motion
for a single subject---in each case camera coordination or control is centered
on and relative to the subject's position often via a circular formation
consisting of 3--4 aerial vehicles.
These systems could hypothetically be adapted to capture multiple subjects such
as by fitting a circle around the group and constructing a formation around the
center.
However, if subjects spread apart, formation schemes will eventually fail.
Thus, these prior works do not address challenges related to
coordinating multiple cameras to film subjects that may spread apart or form and
reform groups.
Nor do these works address control of camera intrinsics such as zoom.

Further, while the challenge of aerial motion capture is compelling,
aerial platforms do not necessarily provide a desirable format for most motion
capture applications.
Limits on battery life lead to constraints on operation time or else
logistical challenges, and
operators must address safety concerns for subjects being filmed as well as
policy constraints.
Development of a more economical class of systems that provides similar
affordances---operation in large and outdoor volumes with minimal
infrastructure---is desirable.

\paragraph{Pan-tilt-zoom motion capture}

Rather than controlling the locations of aerial cameras to follow the subject or
subjects, consider directing the fields-of-view of a small number of
fixed cameras to observe and reconstruct motions of subjects as they
move about a contained volume~\citep{bachmann20193dv,yamamoto2022visigrapp}.
\citet{bachmann20193dv} developed one such
markerless motion capture system with pan-tilt-zoom (PTZ) cameras at fixed
locations but with uncertain orientations, and they present application and
results for tracking skeletal pose of a downhill skier.
Yet, there has been little interest beyond these few works in developing
markerless motion capture capabilities based on PTZ cameras.

These prior works based on PTZ cameras and likewise those built around aerial
vehicles equipped analogously with gimbal cameras demonstrate feasibility of
tracking and reconstructing kinematic motion with moving cameras.
Yet still, neither body of work addresses the general problem of coordinating
viewpoints and zoom for multiple cameras to film multiple subjects.
Nor do they address challenges related to common human motions
such as a group of athletes that comes together, spreads apart, and reorganizes
over the course of a play.

\subsection{Gap: Camera coordination}

Camera coordination for multi-perspective filming tasks has not yet been
studied extensively.
Both aerial and pan-tilt-zoom motion capture methods adopt
representations that center a single
subject~\citep{ho2021iros,saini2022ral,saini2019iccv,hong2024eccv,zhou2018icra,bachmann20193dv,yamamoto2022visigrapp}.
On the other hand, methods for multi-robot active perception or information
gathering~\citep{singh2009,corah2019auro,atanasov2015icra} address theoretic and
practical challenges related to coordinating multiple sensors or cameras
\emph{but not often for the purpose of multi-perspective filming}; rather existing
works emphasize problems such as
sampling spatio-temporal phenomena~\citep{singh2009},
localizing targets~\cite{atanasov2015icra}, or
mapping three-dimensional environments~\citep{corah2019auro}.

\subsection{Contributions}

Our recent contributions develop methods for coordination of multiple cameras
that are suitable for filming
tasks~\citep{hughes2024cdc,suresh2024iros,bouy2026}.
The primary contribution of our proposed work will be to
\textbf{1)} apply these recent
advances in camera coordination to the task of markerless motion capture.
Doing so will
\textbf{2)} enable filming and reconstructing motions of multiple subjects
and with complex movement patterns in contrast to prior works.
We will also promote continued development of this class of systems via
\textbf{3)} dataset and open source software releases.


\section{Methods}

We propose developing a markerless motion capture system based on a small number
of pan tilt zoom cameras (e.g. 4--8) that can autonomously track multiple
subjects moving together and separately over a large space such as a football
field or running track.
A key part of this approach will involve integration of assignment-free view
planning methods we have developed for this multi-camera, multi-subject setting
based on submodular optimization
methods~\citep{suresh2024iros,hughes2024cdc,bouy2026}.

\subsection{Multi-camera coordination}

Autonomous camera view planning is a key element of our approach.
Our prior works on this topic present methods to optimize and maximize pixel
densities over subjects' surfaces for multiple cameras
jointly~\citep{suresh2024iros,hughes2024cdc,bouy2026}.
This approach applies submodular optimization methods to maximize view quality
directly without relying on fixed assignments and guaranteeing constant-factor
suboptimality for approximate solutions to the joint multi-camera optimization
problem.
Simulation results involving a team of UAVs filming multiple moving subjects
demonstrated emergent assignment and formation behaviors and superior
performance compared to formation methods when subjects split and rejoin
(Fig.~\ref{fig:multi-robot-videography}).
We propose applying a similar approach to planning for pan-tilt-zoom cameras by
reformulating the earlier planning problem according to the PTZ camera configuration
space.

\subsection{Reconstruction}

Having developed a camera controller that autonomously obtains views of subjects
from multiple perspectives, we plan to adopt similar methods for skeletal
reconstruction and markerless motion capture as have been demonstrated
previously for systems consisting of multiple PTZ
cameras~\citep{bachmann20193dv} or
UAVs~\citep{saini2019iccv,ho2021iros,hong2024eccv,saini2022ral}.
Our contributions in this area will focus on mitigating effects of uncertainty
in extrinsics and intrinsics due to camera motion and investigating limits on
precision and functional capacity for the prototype system.

\subsection{Systems and experimental validation}

register the coordinate frames of the two motion capture systems by precisely
locating cameras with surveying equipment (e.g. by using a total station).
We will also leverage these experiments to develop and release datasets suitable
for continued development and improvement of reconstruction and motion capture
methods for skeletal and dense reconstruction.

\subsection{Future work and proposed extensions}

During the initial one-year timeline, our research efforts will be limited in
scope to reconstruction of human skeletal pose.
If this project is extended for another year, we expect to focus on enabling
dense motion capture and reconstruction.
Methods for dense reconstruction of dynamic environments, e.g. methods for
dynamic Gaussian splatting~\citep{zheng2025cvpr}, are quickly making dense
dynamic reconstruction more accessible.
However, current methods are largely limited to laboratory environments and
may require additional instrumentation to compute accurate depth
measurements~\citep{zheng2025cvpr}.

\micah{More plausible use of depth and reconstruction}
We expect our activities would focus on developing systems for dense
reconstruction and addressing subsequent challenges for planning and autonomy.
From a systems standpoint, achieving dense reconstruction with PTZ cameras
may leverage models for metric depth estimation~\citep{ke2024cvpr}
but will likely still involve challenging registration and optimization
problems.
However, developing such capabilities would enable integration with
state-of-art methods for novel view synthesis with dynamic scenes such as based
on Gaussian splatting~\citep{zheng2025cvpr}.
Moreover, we expect dense, dynamic reconstruction to exacerbate challenges
related to \emph{resolution} and \emph{coverage}.
At the same time, a strength of our approach is that because we model and
optimize pixel densities over the surface of the scene directly, our methods are
therefore suitable to incorporating these twin challenges into our design and
modeling.
In this sense, coverage and resolution are requirements---we can adapt our
planning approach accordingly (utility design and problem formulation) and
design camera systems (numbers, placements, and models of cameras) to meet
requirements for dense reconstruction.

Dr. Corah is an Assistant Professor of Computer Science at the Colorado
School of Mines and director of the
Navigation, Aerial-robots, and Perception Planning Lab (NAPPLab).\footnote{%
  NAPPLab: \url{https://www.napplab.org/}
}
Dr. Corah has extensive experience in the area of active perception and especially
regarding optimization for coordination of multiple
cameras~\citep{corah2019auro,corah2018cdc,corah2021iros}.
Recently, Dr. Corah has become interested in applying such methods to tasks that
involve filming multiple moving subjects (athletes, performers) from multiple
perspectives~\citep{suresh2024iros,hughes2024cdc,bouy2026}.
These contributions form the basis for this proposal.

Dr. Corah's work also demonstrates his interest in the relationship between
representations of three-dimensional environments and autonomous coordination of
camera motions to produce those representations, especially via the process of
robot exploration or autonomous
mapping~\citep{corah2019ral,corah2021icra,corah2019auro,goel2019fsr,kim2023ral}.
In particular, this interest led to contributions toward Gaussian mixture
mapping~\citep{corah2019ral} preceding the advent and popularity of Gaussian
splatting methods for reconstruction.

Dr. Corah also has a background in development and deployment of robotic mobility
and camera systems.
As a postdoctoral researcher at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Dr. Corah competed
with team CoSTAR in the final stage of the DARPA Subterranean
Challenge~\citep{morrell2024addendum}.
This competition involved field deployment of multi-robot teams to search and
explore underground environments representative of caves, mines, and urban
underground.
Dr. Corah's contributions to team CoSTAR are representative of a longstanding
interest in deployment of robotic perception systems---especially single- and
multi-robot drone systems for exploration of
three-dimensional environments~\citep{corah2019auro,kim2023ral,goel2019fsr}.
Dr. Corah will draw on his experience with development of robotic perception systems
and logistics for robot field deployments while supervising the proposed work.
